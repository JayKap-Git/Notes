1Why study karma at all

Karma is the "behind the scenes" of every experience we have.

From class: the phone call

A dials B and they talk. That is all A sees. But behind the scene there is a huge amount going on: how the call gets connected, the network, the towers, the routing. Our life is the same. We see only "this happened to me". Karma is the hidden network that decided why it happened.

The slides open with a pointed remark: people generally talk about karma more and Krishna less. Karma has become a popular word, but in the Gita it is not the main subject. It is the problem; Krishna is the solution.

Karma applies only to conditioned souls

Karma is a subject only for conditioned living entities: souls who have rebelled against the Lord and want to enjoy independently of Him. A soul who is not in rebellion is not under the law of karma at all.

Your notes draw this with the city and jail picture used earlier in the course:

  • Spiritual world = the city. Souls there are not misusing their facilities, so they are given full freedom.
  • Material world = the jail. Souls here are constrained, that is, conditioned. Just as many rules and laws apply to a prisoner that don't apply to a free citizen, many rules (karma among them) apply to the jīva in the material world.
  • Why did the soul come down? Because of icchā (desire, to enjoy separately) and dveṣa (envy of the Lord). The ×3 and ×2 marks in your sketch show souls in the spiritual world and the few who fell.
Remember
  • Action does not necessarily mean karma. Some action creates no karma at all (that is akarma, §9).
  • Karma is a feature of the jail, not of the soul's real home.

2What karma is

Karma is action that builds your next body.

Bhagavad Gita 8.3
śrī-bhagavān uvāca
akṣaraṁ brahma paramaṁ svabhāvo 'dhyātmam ucyate
bhūta-bhāvodbhava-karo visargaḥ karma-saṁjñitaḥ

The Supreme Personality of Godhead said: The indestructible, transcendental living entity is called Brahman, and his eternal nature is called adhyātma, the self. Action pertaining to the development of the material bodies of the living entities is called karma, or fruitive activities.

This is the textbook definition. Three terms are defined in one verse: the soul is Brahman, its eternal nature is adhyātma, and karma is whatever action leads to the development of material bodies. In your notes: karma is what keeps you trapped in the birth–death cycle through the 84 lakh (8.4 million) species.

The working definition

  • Karma = actions that lead to the development of future bodies; activities that bring some reaction.
  • Akarma (inaction) = actions that do not bear any reaction.

So the test is not "did I do something?" but "does this action come back to me as a reaction, in this body or the next?"

Bhagavad Gita 4.16 · Even the intelligent are bewildered
kiṁ karma kim akarmeti kavayo 'py atra mohitāḥ
tat te karma pravakṣyāmi yaj jñātvā mokṣyase 'śubhāt

Even the intelligent are bewildered in determining what is action and what is inaction. Now I shall explain to you what action is, knowing which you shall be liberated from all misfortune.

Your notes title this "Discrimination is not easy." Even kavayaḥ, the wise, get confused. That is why we need Krishna's explanation, and the promise is big: understand this properly and you are freed from all misfortune (aśubhāt).

3The law of karma

A law of God's mercy, not of God's anger.

The first thing to understand

Your notes underline it in red: no one else is responsible for the reactions we are getting. We get exactly what our own choices earned.

  • Free will. The Lord has given every jīva free will. Used properly, it takes us back to the spiritual world. Misused, we suffer. (Class example: the Lord created the world and gave free will; it's the jīva who chooses alcohol or drugs.)
  • Icchā and dveṣa. All jīvas in the material world want to be the best and grab as much as they can. The goal becomes "facilities".
  • So the world can never be peaceful. It is full of jīvas with desire and envy, each trying to acquire more resources than they need. Resources are limited, so jīvas fight, hurt and harm each other, and then the reactions come. Class example: one country wanting to capture another's land, and that one wanting a third's.

Why it is called God's mercy (compassion)

From class: the father and ten sons

A father has ten sons. He buys ten apples and gives one to each child. One son snatches the apples of two or three brothers and beats them. The father punishes that child. He does it not out of envy, only out of love, and he still loves that son as much as the others.

That is the law of karma. The punishment is corrective, meant to reform the jīva and bring it back, like a jail meant to reform rather than to take revenge. So the idea that "the Lord is a punishment-giver" is a wrong concept.

  • The law of karma is maintained by the demigods. The Lord does not interfere with this law.
  • "I didn't know" is no excuse. "I am very famous / powerful" is no excuse. The law treats everyone the same.
From class: fire

Whether a child or an adult touches fire, both get burned, no matter who they are. The child should have learned from his parents not to touch fire. The fire won't excuse him because he didn't know that it burns.

The law also proves the law-giver

The notes box this as an argument for God's existence: a law means a law-maker; imposing a law means a law-imposer. A system this precise, which tracks every act across lifetimes, implies an intelligence behind it.

Remember
  • We get results according to desire and deserve: what we wanted, and what we earned.
  • The law is compassionate, impartial and corrective.

4Three kinds of action

Sakarma, vikarma and akarma, and why the first two are both a problem.

Bhagavad Gita 4.17
karmaṇo hy api boddhavyaṁ boddhavyaṁ ca vikarmaṇaḥ
akarmaṇaś ca boddhavyaṁ gahanā karmaṇo gatiḥ

The intricacies of action are very hard to understand. Therefore one should know properly what action is, what forbidden action is, and what inaction is.

Gahanā karmaṇo gatiḥ: the way of karma is deep, like a dense forest. Krishna names all three categories here, and says each one must be "known" (boddhavyam) separately.

One action, three outcomes: Arjuna

The slides use Arjuna to show that the category depends on how and why you act, not just on what you do. Fighting is Arjuna's prescribed duty as a kṣatriya.

  • If Arjuna begs for a living instead, it is vikarma (he abandons his duty).
  • If Arjuna fights, it is karma.
  • If Arjuna fights for the pleasure of Vishnu, it is akarma.

Dry stool and wet stool

Karma (puṇya) and vikarma (pāpa) both bring reactions in the form of a future body. The slides ask: dry stool or wet stool, which is better? The dry one is less messy, but you wouldn't want either. Puṇya is relatively better than pāpa, and pāpa is really bad, but puṇya is not spiritual. A golden chain still binds.

Puṇya and pāpa don't cancel

You cannot neutralise puṇya with pāpa or pāpa with puṇya. Each has its own separate reactions, which have to be experienced.

From class: two quivers

Your notes draw two separate rows of arrows, one labelled S (sakarma) and one V (vikarma). They are two separate accounts. Doing a lot of charity does not erase a theft; you will enjoy the reward of the charity and suffer the reaction of the theft, each in its own time.

5Four kinds of reaction

Every sakarma or vikarma leaves four kinds of trace behind.

ReactionMeaningWhat it looks like
PrārabdhaManifested reaction: what has already come to fruit.Specific bodily features: short or tall, beautiful or ugly, dull or sharp intelligence; the situation one is born in, rich or poor. Your notes: hard to change.
AprārabdhaUnmanifested reaction: in stock, yet to manifest.When there is such a stock, one feels distress and anxiety for no reason. Class link: the mental disorders common in Kali-yuga. It is "stored up", and when the opportunity comes, the reaction happens.
KūṭaSinful or pious inclination; the start of a desire.Doing an activity gives a tendency to do it again. Such tendencies accumulate and form a desire. Your notes: kūṭa forms through association (saṅgat).
BījaThe desire to do a pious or sinful act; the seed.When one has a desire to do something, he makes plans and efforts to fulfil it. Your notes: keep "hammering" (repeating) an inclination and it becomes bīja, a very strong desire.

Why the cycle never ends on its own

  • Prārabdha and aprārabdha finish with time: you live them out.
  • Kūṭa and bīja cannot be ended by one's own endeavour. The desires lead to further activities, which create new reactions and new desires.
  • So once one does karma, it goes on endlessly.
  • The original karma began with the jīva envying the Supreme Lord. Whatever the jīva does after that carries that tendency, so it can't be overcome by any amount of karma. Good karma doesn't remove the root, because the root is the desire to enjoy separately.
  • Therefore akarma is required. Akarma has the potency to uproot all four: prārabdha, aprārabdha, kūṭa and bīja.
From class: the seed and the phone

Addiction: bīja → plant → more seeds → more plants and seeds. A trap. The jīva is "very, very trapped". The notes connect this to Kali-yuga sitting in our pocket, the phone: every scroll strengthens a tendency (kūṭa) which grows into a craving (bīja).

From class: delayed reactions

Killing an animal: you won't get the reaction immediately. It is stored in aprārabdha. Later you will be born in that scenario, in that animal's body, and you will be killed.

The sleeping dog: a person bursts a cracker next to a sleeping dog and laughs, "ha ha ha". The dog suffers a lot. That karma is stored in aprārabdha, and after many lives the person gets a dog body and goes through similar suffering.

Remember
  • The result of a reaction is not random. It is decided by desire and deserve.
  • Sakarma and vikarma do not cancel each other.

6How subtle the law is

The slides call it "very, very complicated". Class gave six cases.

Dhṛtarāṣṭra's hundred sons

Dhṛtarāṣṭra lost all his hundred sons. The reason: a hundred lives earlier, he had killed a bird's hundred babies on a tree. To undergo the pain of losing a hundred children, he first had to go through a hundred lives and acquire enough puṇya to get a hundred sons. Only then could the reaction come, through the killing of his hundred sons. This shows how precisely, and over how long a time, the law works.

When X kills B: whose karma is it?

The question raised in class on 20 March: if B was destined to suffer (it was B's karma), should X be punished?

  • You can't enjoy or suffer more than is there in your karma. So B suffered only what B's karma allowed.
  • But X still gets a reaction, because of his own wrong desire and deed.
  • In short: X had the desire, and B had the karma. Both are accounted for separately.
From class: the matchbox

Your notes sketch a matchbox, a matchstick and a simple stick. Fire will only ignite when a matchstick is struck on the box; a simple stick won't do it. The way I read the analogy: B's stored karma is like the box, ready to ignite, but it takes someone's active desire (X, the matchstick) to strike it. Neither alone produces the event, and both are responsible for their own part.

A child committing sin

Until about 14 years of age, children don't have that much discrimination. So the reaction for a child's sin goes to the parents, for not training them properly.

Suicide

From class: the library book

A library issues you a book and you burn it. Now you won't get another book. Similarly: say your life is meant to be 50 years and you commit suicide at 30. You were given the human form of life (a facility) to fulfil a purpose. For some time after that you are not given any body; you don't get a gross body and you roam around quite helpless. It is a terrible life: you want to drink water but you can't; you have desires but can't fulfil them.

Giving money

If X gives money to Y and Y misuses it, X also gets a reaction for Y's misuse. Charity has to be given with discrimination, to the right person.

The king's share

A king gets one-sixth of the sinful reactions of his citizens (prajā) and one-sixth of their pious reactions, because he is the one facilitating their lives. That is why it is the duty of a leader to punish people doing sinful activities: if he lets sin flourish, he shares in it.

7Who makes new karma

Only humans. Every other body is for reaping.

Karma can be done only in the human form of life. The human form is called karma-yoni, the body in which new karma is generated. In the higher species (demigods) or the lower species, you only reap the reactions.

Why animals don't create karma

  • Except humans, other species do not generate new karma.
  • They don't have free will, or it is very much curtailed. Their sleep time is fixed, their mating season is fixed. Individual choice is curtailed.
  • They are just reaping the reactions of past sakarma and vikarma.

If you fall, your notes say, you have to go through each and every species in the sequence, and then after thousands and thousands of years you get the human form of life again. The lowest species are the sea trees, plants and shrubs, which don't get even sunlight.

What about heaven?

As long as the jīva is in the material world, he is trapped. If his sakarma is very good he can go to the heavenly planets: better facilities, no old age, no troubles. But after finishing his quota of puṇya, he falls back to earth. And there is always the possibility of falling into bad association again. Heaven is a better cell in the same jail.

What is out of our control

From class: three things you can't control
  • Once you submit your paper, the ball is in the examiner's court.
  • After you sleep, what you dream is not in your control.
  • After you die, which next life you get is not in your control.

The point: we have choice only while acting in the human body. The result, and the next body, are decided by the law.

8Why not just stop working?

If every action binds, the obvious idea is to do nothing. It doesn't work.

Since every activity has stringent reactions, some think it's better not to do any work: leave everything and go to the forest. But in the forest too, staying in a cave, one develops a tendency (a desire) to stay in the cave. Work and reactions can't be avoided this way. Akarma means inaction, but inaction doesn't mean no work.

From class

The nature we have is accumulated from so many past lives, so one can't change oneself in just one night. Even if you go to the forest, you will get bored and come back.

What Chapter 3 says (from your notes)

  • 3.4: Not by renouncing alone can one attain perfection.
  • 3.5: Everyone is forced to act helplessly according to the qualities acquired from the modes of material nature, so no one can refrain from doing something, even for a moment.
  • 3.6: Even if you restrain the senses, you can't stop thinking about the sense objects. (Outer renunciation with inner craving is pretence.)
  • 3.7: The sincere person practises karma-yoga: controlling the senses by the mind and working without attachment.
Bhagavad Gita 3.8 · Prescribed duties
niyataṁ kuru karma tvaṁ karma jyāyo hy akarmaṇaḥ
śarīra-yātrāpi ca te na prasiddhyed akarmaṇaḥ

Perform your prescribed duty, for doing so is better than not working. One cannot even maintain one's physical body without work.

Notice that akarmaṇaḥ here means simply "not working", which is different from the technical akarma of 4.17. Krishna's verdict is clear: doing your duty beats doing nothing, and you can't even keep the body alive without action.

9Akarma and yajña

The way out is to change who the work is for.

Bhagavad Gita 3.9
yajñārthāt karmaṇo 'nyatra loko 'yaṁ karma-bandhanaḥ
tad-arthaṁ karma kaunteya mukta-saṅgaḥ samācara

Work done as a sacrifice for Vishnu has to be performed; otherwise work causes bondage in this material world. Therefore, O son of Kuntī, perform your prescribed duties for His satisfaction, and in that way you will always remain free from bondage.

The key verse of the topic. Your notes compress it to two lines: work for the Lord → akarma; work for the fruits → sakarma. Only yajñārtha work, work as sacrifice for Vishnu, escapes karma-bandhana (bondage by karma).

What akarma is

  • Action done only for the pleasure of Vishnu.
  • It doesn't bear any material results.
  • It has the potency to uproot all four types of accumulated reactions: prārabdha, aprārabdha, kūṭa and bīja. Your notes: it is so potent that it can burn off all of one's sakarma and vikarma.

Yajña

Yajña means any activity done for the pleasure of Vishnu. Vishnu is also known as Yajña-puruṣa, or simply Yajña. Acting for His pleasure requires selflessness, and acting selflessly is not easy. That is why we need a spiritual master.

Bhagavad Gita 3.14 · Yajña and prescribed duties
annād bhavanti bhūtāni parjanyād anna-sambhavaḥ
yajñād bhavati parjanyo yajñaḥ karma-samudbhavaḥ

All living bodies subsist on food grains, which are produced from rains. Rains are produced by performance of yajña [sacrifice], and yajña is born of prescribed duties.

A chain: prescribed duty → yajña → rain → grain → living bodies. The phrase the slides pick out is yajñaḥ karma-samudbhavaḥ, "yajña is born of prescribed duties". You don't do yajña by leaving your duty; you do it by performing your duty as a sacrifice.

Who decides my prescribed duty?

  • Duties are prescribed in the Vedic scriptures.
  • But someone still has to prescribe them according to the person's nature. This needs a spiritual relationship with a spiritual master.
  • Performing an act for the Lord's pleasure is possible only when one has a relationship with Him. Therefore yoga is required (taken up later in the course).
Bhagavad Gita 2.47 · Yajña is non-fruitive
karmaṇy evādhikāras te mā phaleṣu kadācana
mā karma-phala-hetur bhūr mā te saṅgo 'stv akarmaṇi

You have a right to perform your prescribed duty, but you are not entitled to the fruits of action. Never consider yourself the cause of the results of your activities, and never be attached to not doing your duty.

Four instructions in one verse: (1) your right is to the work; (2) not to the fruit; (3) don't think you are the cause of results; (4) don't become attached to inaction either.

"Work is worship"

The popular saying karm hi pūjā hai is true only in a specific sense. Which work is worship? Only a prescribed duty done with the sole motivation of pleasing Lord Vishnu. Claiming proprietorship over the results doesn't make work worship. Work should be done without desire for any fruits, and the only desire should be to bring happiness to the Supreme Lord.

Definition to memorise

Yajña means doing prescribed duty without attachment to the fruits of one's work and performing it only for the pleasure of Lord Vishnu.

10The four conditions for akarma

From your notes. All four have to be present.

1It must be a prescribed duty
2Done without attachment to results
3With the understanding "I am not the doer"
4Done for the pleasure of the Lord

1 · It must be a prescribed duty

You can't make any action akarma just by saying "it's for God". Drinking alcohol to please the Lord ✗. Killing people to please the Lord ✗. The action itself must be sanctioned.

From class: the map of India

A soldier who kills an enemy at the border gets a medal. A man who kills someone inside the country goes to prison. The same outward act, killing, is duty in one case and crime in the other. What decides it is whether the act is prescribed.

Verses cited: 3.8 (perform your prescribed duty, §8) and 16.23, which says that one who discards scriptural injunctions and acts on whim attains neither perfection, nor happiness, nor the supreme destination.

2 · Without attachment to results (BG 2.47)

From class: the bank cashier

A cashier counts lakhs of rupees every day but isn't attached to that money, or to the bank's profit or loss. आज कम गिना, आज ज़्यादा ("today I counted less, today more"): it makes no difference to him. He does his job fully and leaves the result where it belongs.

3 · "I am not the doer" (BG 3.27)

Don't take the credit. Out of false ego, the soul thinks it is doing everything, when activities are actually carried out by the three modes of material nature. The full analysis is in §11.

Bhagavad Gita 3.27
prakṛteḥ kriyamāṇāni guṇaiḥ karmāṇi sarvaśaḥ
ahaṅkāra-vimūḍhātmā kartāham iti manyate

The spirit soul bewildered by the influence of false ego thinks himself the doer of activities that are in actuality carried out by the three modes of material nature.

4 · For the pleasure of the Lord (BG 3.9)

Your notes give the clearest meaning of "sacrifice" in 3.9: it is a sacrifice of the fruitive mentality. Whenever we act, we have the desire to enjoy the result ("I want to enjoy"). An action done to please the Lord is called a sacrifice because we are sacrificing the desire to enjoy the result of the action.

From class: grapes

You have beautiful grapes and offer them to the Lord. Not because God has a scarcity of grapes, but because this strengthens your relationship with Him. "These are such beautiful grapes, let me offer them to the Lord", without the tendency to enjoy them first yourself.

Same actionWhere the focus is
SakarmaFocus is on I: my enjoyment, my result.
AkarmaFocus is on the Supreme Lord and on going back to the spiritual world. It's not that you will lose out materially by having this mentality.
From class (21 March): teaching the Gita

Even a pious activity like teaching the Bhagavad Gita can be either kind. Done just to complete my hours or for a grade, it is sakarma. Done to get free from the cycle of birth and death, it is akarma.

Note: the slides define akarma strictly as action done for Krishna's pleasure. "To get free from birth and death" in the class example is best read the same way: freedom comes as the result of working for Him. In an exam, phrase it in terms of pleasing the Lord.

Your notes on 21 March tick "prescribed duty" as the easy one and circle "without attachment to result" and "I am not the doer" as the hard parts. That is why the slides say acting selflessly is not easy and needs a guide.

11The five factors of action

Why I am not the only doer, and so have no claim on the result.

Bhagavad Gita 18.13
pañcaitāni mahā-bāho kāraṇāni nibodha me
sāṅkhye kṛtānte proktāni siddhaye sarva-karmaṇām

O mighty-armed Arjuna, according to the Vedānta there are five causes for the accomplishment of all action. Now learn of these from Me.

Bhagavad Gita 18.14
adhiṣṭhānaṁ tathā kartā karaṇaṁ ca pṛthag-vidham
vividhāś ca pṛthak ceṣṭā daivaṁ caivātra pañcamam

The place of action [the body], the performer, the various senses, the many different kinds of endeavor, and ultimately the Supersoul—these are the five factors of action.

  1. Place of action (the body).
  2. Performer (the doer, the soul).
  3. Senses: your notes add that they are superior to the body.
  4. Different kinds of endeavour, and your notes stress: not just yours.
  5. The Supersoul, who finally sanctions the action.
From class: whose endeavour?
  • The mess workers cooked food for you, and only then were you able to give your exam.
  • Babulal ji set up the mic, so sir could deliver the lecture.
  • If other people didn't get lower marks, how would you get an A? If you got an A, so many people contributed to it.

śarīra-vāṅ-manobhir yat karma prārabhate naraḥ
nyāyyaṁ vā viparītaṁ vā pañcaite tasya hetavaḥ

Whatever right or wrong action a man performs by body, mind or speech is caused by these five factors.

Bhagavad Gita 18.16
tatraivaṁ sati kartāram ātmānaṁ kevalaṁ tu yaḥ

Therefore one who thinks himself the only doer, not considering the five factors, is certainly not very intelligent and cannot see things as they are.

This is the answer to "why not desire the results of work?" If I am one of five causes, and the final one is the Supersoul, then claiming the result as "mine" is simply a wrong view of reality.

12Offering the results of work

What pleases the Lord is the affection behind the offering.

Bhagavad Gita 3.30
mayi sarvāṇi karmāṇi sannyasyādhyātma-cetasā
nirāśīr nirmamo bhūtvā yudhyasva vigata-jvaraḥ

Therefore, O Arjuna, surrendering all your works unto Me, with full knowledge of Me, without desires for profit, with no claims to proprietorship, and free from lethargy, fight.

Five qualities in one line: surrender all works to Me · with knowledge of Me (adhyātma-cetasā) · without desire for profit (nirāśīḥ) · without "mine-ness" (nirmamaḥ) · free from lethargy or feverish anxiety (vigata-jvaraḥ).

The jīva can hardly offer anything to God; everything already belongs to Him. What pleases the Lord is the affection the jīva shows towards Him.

From class: the child's painting

A child says, "See, papa, I made a painting!" The father says, "Arre wah!", even though the child has only drawn a few lines, a very rough drawing. What pleases the father is the affection the child has towards him.

Changing the fruitive mindset

Your notes draw the jīva at the centre of its world, seeing everything with a fruitive mindset: food, a girl, and so on. Everything is "how can I enjoy this?". We came into the material world with the desire to enjoy separately from the Lord, so this mindset is very difficult to change.

But if the jīva gets the message of the Gita, it can understand: "I've got this rare human form of life; birth and death are real; so let me work to please the Supreme Lord." Then we dovetail our endeavours so that they take us closer to the Supreme Lord. Nothing is given up outwardly; the direction changes.

13The peace formula

Three facts about Krishna which, once known, bring peace.

Bhagavad Gita 5.29
bhoktāraṁ yajña-tapasāṁ sarva-loka-maheśvaram
suhṛdaṁ sarva-bhūtānāṁ jñātvā māṁ śāntim ṛcchati

14One's quota

Take what your duty needs, and no more.

Śrī Īśopaniṣad, Mantra 1
īśāvāsyam idaṁ sarvaṁ yat kiñca jagatyāṁ jagat
tena tyaktena bhuñjīthā mā gṛdhaḥ kasya svid dhanam

Everything animate or inanimate that is within the universe is controlled and owned by the Lord. One should therefore accept only those things necessary for himself, which are set aside as his quota, and one should not accept other things, knowing well to whom they belong.

  • Everything, animate and inanimate, belongs to the Supreme Lord.
  • One should accept only those things necessary for carrying out one's prescribed duty.
  • Accepting more than necessary is dishonesty, and everything one does with that dishonesty is bad karma only.

This links back to §3: the world is never peaceful because every jīva grabs more than its quota. The Īśopaniṣad's answer is the cure for that.

15Different platforms of worship

The same worship can come from four different motives.

PlatformMotive
FearOne worships out of fear of something going wrong.
DesireOne worships expecting some material result in return.
DutyOne worships out of scriptural obligation.
LoveOne worships out of selfless love, with the only motivation of giving happiness.

The list runs from lowest to highest. Only the last is fully akarma: the first two are still fruitive (I worship to avoid a loss or to get a gain), and the third is good but mechanical. The child's painting in §12 is the platform of love.

Giving up the work, or giving up the fruit.

This closes the loop with §8: running to the forest is an attempt at sannyāsa; the Gita's recommendation is tyāga, which is the same thing as akarma in practice.

17One-page summary

The whole topic in the order you'd write it in an answer.

  1. Definition (8.3): karma is action that leads to the development of material bodies. Akarma is action with no reaction.
  2. Scope: karma applies only to conditioned souls who rebelled against the Lord (icchā, dveṣa). City vs jail.
  3. Hard to understand (4.16, 4.17): even the wise are bewildered; know karma, vikarma and akarma.
  4. The law: no one else is responsible for our reactions; free will; God's mercy (father and sons); maintained by demigods; ignorance or power is no excuse (fire); law implies a law-maker; results by desire and deserve.
  5. Three kinds: sakarma (by scripture), vikarma (against scripture or neglecting duty), akarma (for Krishna). Arjuna example. Dry and wet stool. They don't cancel.
  6. Four reactions: prārabdha, aprārabdha, kūṭa, bīja. The first two finish with time; the last two keep the cycle going. Original karma is envy of the Lord, so no karma can fix it.
  7. Subtlety: Dhṛtarāṣṭra's 100 sons; X had the desire, B had the karma; child under 14; suicide; giving money; king's one-sixth.
  8. Human birth = karma-yoni. Other species and demigods only reap. Heaven ends.
  9. Renouncing doesn't work (3.4–3.8): no one can stay without action; inaction doesn't mean no work.
  10. Akarma = yajña (3.9, 3.14, 2.47): prescribed duty, without attachment, for Vishnu's pleasure. Uproots all four reactions. Needs a spiritual master.
  11. Four conditions: prescribed duty · no attachment · not the doer · for the Lord.
  12. Not the doer (3.27, 18.13–16): five factors: body, soul, senses, endeavours, Supersoul.
  13. Offering (3.30): affection is what pleases Him (child's painting).
  14. Peace formula (5.29): beneficiary, proprietor, friend. Knowing vs being aware.

18Exam-style questions

Try each one before opening the answer. Tagged ones came up in the past papers you went through earlier.

How is karma defined in the Bhagavad Gita? Past paper

BG 8.3: "Action pertaining to the development of the material bodies of the living entities is called karma, or fruitive activities." Karma is action that brings a reaction and so leads to a future body. Akarma is action that bears no reaction.

How is the law of karma an expression of God's compassion? Past paper

The law is impartial: everyone gets results only according to their own desire and deserve, and no one else is responsible. It is also corrective rather than vengeful. Like a father who punishes the son who snatched his brothers' apples, out of love and still loving him equally, the law reforms the jīva so it can return to the Lord. The Lord does not interfere; demigods maintain the law. "God as punishment-giver" is a wrong concept.

Explain prārabdha, aprārabdha, kūṭa and bīja. Past paper
  • Prārabdha: manifested reaction: bodily features, intelligence, birth situation.
  • Aprārabdha: unmanifested, stocked reaction; felt as anxiety without reason; comes when the opportunity arises.
  • Kūṭa: inclination; an act creates a tendency to repeat it, built up by association.
  • Bīja: desire; repeated inclination becomes a strong desire that makes plans and efforts.

The first two end with time. The last two cannot be ended by one's own effort and produce new acts, so the cycle continues. Only akarma uproots all four.

Distinguish sakarma, vikarma and akarma with examples. Past paper
  • Sakarma: action by scriptural rules, impelled by the modes, for one's own result: charity, feeding cows, Gaṅgā snān. Gives puṇya and a future body.
  • Vikarma: action against scripture, or neglecting duty: cheating, stealing, intoxication, neglecting old parents. Gives pāpa and a future body.
  • Akarma: prescribed duty done without attachment for Vishnu's pleasure. No reaction.

Arjuna: begging = vikarma; fighting = karma; fighting for Vishnu's pleasure = akarma.

Why are sakarma and vikarma compared to dry and wet stool? Past paper

Both are stool: one is less messy, but neither is desirable. Likewise both puṇya and pāpa bring a future material body and keep the soul in birth and death. Puṇya is better than pāpa but is not spiritual. Only akarma frees.

"I'm fine just doing good karma." What is the problem? Past paper

Sakarma still produces reactions to be enjoyed in another body, so you stay in the cycle. Even heaven ends: after the puṇya is used up you fall back to earth, with the risk of bad association. And the root, the original envy of the Lord, is untouched, so kūṭa and bīja keep generating new karma. No amount of karma removes karma; akarma is required.

Can puṇya cancel pāpa? How can stocked karma be burnt? Past paper

No. Puṇya and pāpa have separate reactions, each of which must be experienced (two separate quivers of arrows). Stocked karma is burnt only by akarma, work done as yajña for the pleasure of Vishnu, which has the potency to uproot prārabdha, aprārabdha, kūṭa and bīja.

X kills B. If it was B's karma to suffer, why should X be punished?

No one can suffer or enjoy more than their karma allows, so B suffered only what was due. But X acted out of his own wrong desire and deed, which creates his own reaction. X had the desire; B had the karma. Each is accounted for separately (matchstick and matchbox).

Why don't animals generate new karma?

Their free will is absent or very curtailed: their sleep and mating are fixed by nature. They are only reaping past reactions. Only the human body is karma-yoni. Demigods too only reap.

If every action binds, why not give up all action and go to the forest?

It is impossible to stay without action even for a moment (3.5); even the body can't be maintained without work (3.8); restraining the senses while thinking of their objects is pretence (3.6). Even in a cave one develops attachment to the cave. Our nature comes from many lives and doesn't change overnight. Inaction (akarma) doesn't mean no work; it means work for Vishnu (3.9). Tyāga, giving up fruits, is better than sannyāsa, giving up work.

What are the four conditions for work to be akarma?
  1. It must be a prescribed duty (not alcohol or killing "for God"; soldier's medal vs murderer's prison; 3.8, 16.23).
  2. Without attachment to results (2.47; the bank cashier).
  3. Understanding "I am not the doer" (3.27; the five factors, 18.14).
  4. For the pleasure of the Lord (3.9; sacrifice of the fruitive mentality; grapes).
What are the five factors of action, and what follows from them?

BG 18.14: the body (place), the performer (soul), the senses, the various endeavours (including others', like the mess workers), and the Supersoul. Since the soul is only one of five causes, one who thinks himself the sole doer "cannot see things as they are" (18.16). So there's no basis to claim the result as mine.

State the peace formula and its three parts.

BG 5.29. Knowing Krishna as (1) the ultimate beneficiary of all sacrifices and austerities, since nothing happens without His sanction; (2) the Supreme Lord and proprietor of all planets, since only He owns past, present and future; (3) the friend and well-wisher of all living beings. One who knows this attains peace. For work to be akarma, one must be aware of these, not just know them.

What does "work is worship" really mean?

Only prescribed duty done with the sole motivation of pleasing Vishnu, without claiming the results, is worship. Work done for oneself, even good work, is not worship. Knowing one's prescribed duty requires a spiritual master.

Explain one's quota from Īśopaniṣad 1.

Everything belongs to the Lord. Accept only what is needed to carry out your prescribed duty. Accepting more is dishonesty, and everything done with it is bad karma.

Name the four platforms of worship.

Fear (something may go wrong), desire (material gain), duty (scriptural obligation), and love (selfless, only to give Him happiness).
